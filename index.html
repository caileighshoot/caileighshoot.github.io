MS Thesis Code · R

Forest Type Classification with Hyperspectral + LiDAR

Fused NASA G-LiHT hyperspectral and LiDAR data to classify forest type in the Tanana Valley of interior Alaska, benchmarking five machine learning algorithms. The best model, a Random Forest using hyperspectral vegetation indices, topography, and canopy height, reached 77.53% overall accuracy. This work became my MS thesis and was later published in Remote Sensing.

My Story

Over a decade of working in remote sensing and forestry has taught me that the hardest part usually isn't collecting the data. It's figuring out what to do with it, and turning it into something that people can actually use.

My background spans LiDAR, hyperspectral and multispectral imaging, drones/UAVs, GIS, and data analysis, with experience in both academic research and applied forestry. I started in remote sensing as an undergraduate researcher in September 2014, when I began working on my capstone project with Dr. L. Monika Moskal and Dr. Van Kane. For that project, I investigated whether LiDAR could be used to detect understory vegetation in the sugar pine and mixed-conifer forests of Yosemite National Park. I was also fortunate enough to spend time working in Yosemite as a field research assistant, which gave me an early appreciation for the connection between what we see in the field and what we can measure from above.

In June 2018, I completed my Master of Science in the Remote Sensing and Geospatial Analysis Laboratory at the University of Washington. My thesis, co-chaired by Dr. L. Monika Moskal and Dr. Hans-Erik Andersen, focused on using hyperspectral imaging and LiDAR to classify forest types in the Tanana Valley of interior Alaska. After graduating, I joined NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center as an intern, where I was able to finish processing my thesis data using NASA's supercomputer. The resulting research was published in Remote Sensing in May 2021. More importantly, that experience reinforced something I've carried with me ever since: a remote sensing project isn't really successful just because you can build a good model. It needs to produce results that hold up when someone else tries to use them.

I saw that same challenge from a very different perspective when I moved into industry. Beginning in 2018, I worked in forest remote sensing and GIS at Weyerhaeuser, the largest forestry company in the United States. Over the years, I held roles as a Remote Sensing Research Specialist, Inventory and Planning GIS Analyst, and ultimately Lead Forest Carbon Scientist.

As a Remote Sensing Research Specialist, I worked on integrating remote sensing into Weyerhaeuser's research projects, with a particular focus on LiDAR, multispectral imaging, and UAVs. I ended up logging well over 150 UAV flights, many of them flown solo, and handled everything from flight planning and equipment maintenance to data processing and analysis. It was a very hands-on introduction to what it takes to get remote sensing data from an idea on paper to an actual dataset you can work with.

As an Inventory and Planning GIS Analyst, my work shifted closer to day-to-day forestry operations. I performed GIS mapping and spatial analysis in direct support of operational forestry teams, while also providing user support and training for Esri tools including ArcGIS Desktop, Field Maps, and Survey123, as well as Weyerhaeuser's internal forest management systems and databases. That experience taught me a lot about designing tools and analyses for the people who actually have to use them, rather than just for the people who build them.

I also had the opportunity to return to the University of Washington as a Remote Sensing lecturer in 2022. I filled in for a faculty member for an academic quarter, teaching 115 students part-time while continuing my full-time role at Weyerhaeuser. I really enjoyed teaching and was grateful to have the opportunity to share something I had spent years working with professionally. By the end of the quarter, the course received an overall evaluation score of 4.1 out of 5, with 71% of students responding.

My final role at Weyerhaeuser was as Lead Forest Carbon Scientist, where I combined data analysis in R and Python, GIS, and remote sensing to support the development of high-integrity forest carbon projects. A large part of my work involved building tools and documentation that made complicated processes easier to execute and repeat. One of those tools was an automated decision-support system that ultimately delivered more than $1.3 million in cost savings and received Weyerhaeuser's 2025 Innovator of the Year award.

I'm now a Research Associate at TU Berlin, where I've had the chance to return to research and dive much deeper into questions around forest recovery following large-scale disturbances such as bark beetle outbreaks, windthrow, and wildfire. My current work focuses on how UAV and satellite remote sensing can be combined to better monitor forest recovery and regeneration.

Moving to Germany for this role was also something I'd wanted to do for a long time. I spent my first seven months here in person, learning German and getting a firsthand look at the remote sensing and forestry research community in Europe. Serving on the ForestSAT board has been a big part of that experience as well, giving me the opportunity to connect with researchers and practitioners from around the world and build collaborations I wouldn't have had access to back home.

I'm currently working remotely, as I have for most of the past five years, and travel to Berlin each month to work with my team in person. I'm particularly interested in work at the intersection of remote sensing, environmental data science, and applied forestry, especially where better data can help people make better decisions about forests and the landscapes they manage.
