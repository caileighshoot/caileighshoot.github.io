MS Thesis Code · R

Forest Type Classification with Hyperspectral + LiDAR

I fused NASA G-LiHT hyperspectral and LiDAR data to classify forest type in the Tanana Valley of interior Alaska, benchmarking five machine learning algorithms. The best model, a Random Forest using hyperspectral vegetation indices, topography, and canopy height, hit 77.53% overall accuracy. This became my MS thesis, and it was later published in Remote Sensing.

My Story

Over a decade of working in remote sensing and forestry has taught me that the hardest part usually isn't collecting the data or building the model. It's leading the work: getting the right people and data pointed at the right problem, and turning technical output into something people can actually use.

I started as a technical specialist. As an undergraduate researcher at the University of Washington, I worked on a capstone project with Dr. L. Monika Moskal and Dr. Van Kane, investigating whether LiDAR could detect understory vegetation in the forests of Yosemite National Park, and spent a season there as a field research assistant. That project is where I first got hooked on remote sensing.

I went on to complete my Master of Science in the Remote Sensing and Geospatial Analysis Laboratory at UW, using hyperspectral imaging and LiDAR to classify forest types in interior Alaska, work I finished during an internship at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center and later published in Remote Sensing. That project taught me something I've carried with me since: technical work isn't really finished until it holds up when someone else tries to use it.

I carried that lesson into industry. Starting in 2018, I spent nearly seven years at Weyerhaeuser, the largest forestry company in the US, moving from Remote Sensing Research Specialist to Inventory and Planning GIS Analyst to Lead Forest Carbon Scientist. Each step took me further from doing the technical work myself and closer to leading it: planning UAV programs, designing workflows other people would use, training teams on new systems, and eventually managing a team of two analysts and leading development of tools that supported the whole forest carbon program.

The project I'm proudest of from that time is a decision-support tool I led development of, which took a largely manual forest carbon workflow and turned it into something repeatable and data-driven. It ended up delivering more than $1.3M in projected cost savings and won Weyerhaeuser's 2025 Innovator of the Year award. I also spent a quarter as a part-time lecturer at UW in 2022, teaching 115 students while working full-time, which is where I properly learned how to explain technical work to people who didn't build it.

Now I'm a Research Associate at TU Berlin, where I act as Lead Scientist for the KI-Recover project. It's the most complex thing I've led so far: coordinating technical work across institutions, supervising two student researchers, and planning data acquisition across multiple UAV and satellite sensors to understand how forests recover after large-scale disturbances like bark beetle outbreaks, windthrow, and wildfire.

Moving to Germany for this role was also something I'd wanted to do for a long time. I spent my first seven months here in person, and I'm now fully remote again, as I have been for most of the past five years across industry and academia, traveling to Berlin monthly to work with my team in person. Serving on the ForestSAT board has connected me with researchers and practitioners internationally in a way I didn't have back home.

That progression, from technical specialist to someone leading multidisciplinary technical projects and teams, is the direction I want to keep growing in. I have deep domain expertise in forestry and environmental applications, but I'm just as interested in bringing that technical and project leadership to new problems and industries.
